The amount of change on its own was not enough to tell them apart. You needed the time as well. Putting the two together, how much changed compared with how long it took, is what we call the rate of change.

Finding the pattern

When Can We Trust an Average?

You have seen one average mislead you. Was that a one off, or does it happen for a reason? Judge these five situations, all of them ones you have already met.

A phone left streaming video for an hour, the battery draining faster the hotter it got.

Average drain: 10 points per hour

Does this average tell the whole story, or does it hide something?

A keke on an open road, no stops, covering ten kilometres in twenty minutes.

Average speed: 30 km/h

Does this average tell the whole story, or does it hide something?

There is a pattern. An average can be trusted when the rate holds steady, because then every moment looks like the average. It hides things whenever the rate varies, because the moments no longer match the summary.

Where we have arrived

The Problem We Cannot Yet Solve

Things change› Change has a size› Change takes time› That gives a rate› Rates vary› Averages can mislead

So how fast is something changing at one exact moment?

Every rate you have worked out needed an amount of change and a stretch of time to divide it by. Six litres in six seconds. Twenty points in one minute. Now try it for a single instant, the way a speedometer reads your speed right now.

change in distance ÷ 0 seconds

A single moment has no duration, so there is no interval to divide by. The tool that has served you all the way through this phase runs out here.

Before you see how this is solved: what do you think we could do instead? There is no wrong answer, and this is not graded.

We cannot use an interval of nothing. But we can use an interval that is very small, and then smaller still. Measure across a second, then a tenth of a second, then a hundredth. Each answer gets closer to describing that one moment. The question becomes what those answers settle towards as the interval shrinks, and that is a question we can actually answer.
The answer to that problem

A Precise Language for the Idea

Mathematics already has a way of writing down exactly what you just described. Here it is. Take each part in turn and you will find you have met all of them.

limh → 0 [ f(x + h) − f(x) ] ÷ h

Choose a part of the expression above to see what it means.

Nothing in that expression is new to you. It is the same reasoning you used with the buckets, the phones and the generator, written in a form precise enough to work with. When you meet it again it will have a name, and you will already know what it is for.
